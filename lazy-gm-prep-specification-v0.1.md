# Lazy GM Prep Specification v0.1

## 1. Purpose

This specification defines the default preparation method for GM Prep Help.

Its goal is to produce preparation that is **small, useful at the table, adaptable to player choices, and grounded in the adventure being run**.

The governing principle is:

> Prepare what benefits the game; omit what does not.

This specification is a preparation framework, not a replacement for the published adventure.

---

## 2. Core Principles

### 2.1 Prepare for Play

Prepare material that is likely to help the GM run the next session.

Do not expand notes merely for completeness. Prefer compact, usable information over exhaustive transcription or summary.

### 2.2 Preserve Player Agency

Preparation should support possibilities rather than predict a required sequence of player actions.

Potential scenes, encounters, and developments are possibilities, not a script.

### 2.3 Build Situations, Not Sequences

When practical, frame play around:

- a location;
- inhabitants and what they are doing;
- goals or pressures affecting the characters.

Consider how inhabitants act when left alone and how they react to character actions.

### 2.4 Let the Fiction Determine the Mode of Play

Do not assume in advance that a situation must become combat, social interaction, or exploration.

Let the characters' approach determine what happens.

### 2.5 Fail Forward

A single failed check should not unnecessarily halt the adventure.

Where appropriate, failure should create a complication, cost, changed situation, or new challenge while allowing play to continue.

### 2.6 Prepare Flexible Information

Secrets and clues should normally be prepared independently of a required discovery method or location.

Allow their discovery to emerge through interaction with appropriate NPCs, locations, objects, events, or other circumstances during play.

---

## 3. Source Fidelity for Published Adventures

When preparing a published adventure:

1. **The source adventure is authoritative.**
2. Preserve important facts, relationships, locations, motivations, constraints, and consequences established by the source.
3. Use source page references when they materially help the GM find information during play.
4. Do not silently convert invented prep material into source fact.
5. Clearly distinguish meaningful additions, extrapolations, substitutions, or proposed changes from material established by the source.
6. Customize the adventure for the table without unnecessarily rewriting material the publication has already prepared effectively.

The objective is to **focus and operationalize the source**, not reproduce it.

---

## 4. Default Preparation Framework

Use the Lazy Dungeon Master's eight-step checklist as the default preparation backbone:

1. **Review the Characters**
2. **Create a Strong Start**
3. **Outline Potential Scenes**
4. **Define Secrets and Clues**
5. **Develop Fantastic Locations**
6. **Outline Important NPCs**
7. **Choose Relevant Monsters**
8. **Select Magic Item Rewards**

These are defaults, not mandatory fields.

For a published adventure, some steps may already be adequately supplied by the source and may require only review, selection, or compression. Skip or reduce steps that do not benefit the upcoming session.

---

## 5. Prep Output Requirements

GM Prep Help should normally produce notes that are:

- concise enough to scan during play;
- organized around actionable bullets rather than long prose;
- focused primarily on the next session;
- explicit about likely motivations, pressures, and developments;
- supported by page references where useful;
- selective rather than exhaustive;
- flexible enough to survive unexpected player choices.

Names, goals, clues, consequences, locations, monster behavior, and likely developments generally deserve more space than descriptive retelling.

---

## 6. Strong Starts

The beginning of a session should receive deliberate preparation.

A strong start should rapidly reconnect the players to the game and place the characters into an active situation, meaningful decision, threat, discovery, or interaction.

Do not require the strong start to dictate what happens afterward.

---

## 7. Encounters and Monsters

Choose monsters and NPC opposition because they make sense for the location, situation, and story.

Do not treat every prepared encounter as mandatory combat.

Where useful, prepare:

- likely creature types and quantities;
- motivations and behavior;
- environmental features;
- possible reinforcements, retreats, or reactions;
- major danger or deadliness concerns.

Prefer adaptable encounter ingredients over rigid encounter scripts.

---

## 8. When More Preparation Is Appropriate

Use additional preparation when it clearly benefits play.

Examples include:

- tactically complex set pieces;
- mysteries requiring relationship or clue tracking;
- faction conflicts;
- timelines or countdowns;
- large dungeons;
- wilderness or point-crawl navigation;
- heists;
- political situations;
- complex boss encounters.

The principle remains the same: **prepare the additional structure because it will help at the table, not because completeness requires it.**

---

## 9. Anti-Patterns

Avoid:

- scripting what the characters must do;
- assuming one solution to a situation;
- treating potential scenes as a required sequence;
- hiding necessary progress behind a single successful check;
- rewriting large portions of published text into prep notes;
- preparing distant material with little chance of affecting the next session;
- inventing details without distinguishing them from source material;
- preparing combat encounters solely because a location appears to require a fight;
- adding complexity that is unlikely to improve actual play.

---

## 10. Working Rule

When deciding whether something belongs in GM prep, ask:

**Will this materially help the GM run the game at the table?**

If yes, prepare it at the minimum useful resolution.

If no, omit it.
